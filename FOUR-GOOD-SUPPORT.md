# Ten people for any number of goods if each person cares about at most four

**Written proof, unrefereed.** For each person i suppose there is a set S_i of at most four goods such that u_i(A)=u_i(A∩S_i) for every bundle A. The sets may differ and overlap; the valuations on them may include complementarities and ties. In any prescribed two groups, all ten people admit balanced EF2. There is no bound on the total number of goods. This is a sufficient condition, not an assumption in the original open question.

**Reduce to one triple per person.** A person caring about at most two goods always accepts. For three, receiving any one of the three suffices: the other bundle has at most two relevant goods to delete. For four, select the three highest-valued singletons (break ties arbitrarily). Receiving any one of those three suffices. Indeed, if the other bundle contains three relevant goods, it includes an unselected singleton worth no more than the received one; delete the other two. Otherwise all opposing relevant goods can be deleted. Monotonicity suffices. It is therefore enough that each prescribed group receive at least one good of each triple specified by its members. Equivalently, each triple forbids one of its two possible monochromatic colors.

**Avoid ten signed triples while balancing.** Add an irrelevant dummy for an odd goods count. Write 2k for the even count. For k≤2 the comparison bundle can be deleted outright. For 3≤k≤8 take a uniform k-by-k split. The probability of a particular forbidden event is k(k−1)(k−2)/[(2k)(2k−1)(2k−2)] = (k−2)/[4(2k−1)]. Ten times this is less than one for k<8. At k=8 it equals one, but if there are ten constraints some two forbid the same color. Their union uses at most six goods, so both bad events can occur together with positive probability. The union bound is then strict.

For k≥9 first drop the balance requirement and use independent fair signs. For p triples forbidding all red, avoiding each is a decreasing event in the red indicators; these events are positively correlated by the Harris inequality. The probability of avoiding them all is at least (7/8)^p. Likewise the probability of avoiding the q all-blue events is at least (7/8)^q. Since p+q≤10, their sum is at least 2(7/8)^5 = 33614/32768 > 1. The two avoidance events intersect. This uses positive association *within* each polarity, not between the opposite polarities or on the balanced slice.

Choose a satisfying signing with minimum imbalance. If unbalanced, changing any majority point to the minority color must violate a constraint. There is thus a blocking triple for each majority point: it forbids the all-minority color, and that point is its unique majority member. Different majority points need different triples. For k≥10 an unbalanced majority would have at least eleven points, impossible. For k=9 the only unbalanced possibility is 10:8, with ten different blocking triples and all ten constraints of the same polarity. Two of these triples intersect, since 30 incidences cannot be disjoint on 18 goods. Choose a shared point and one point from each of the other eight triples. Enlarge this hitting set to nine points and color it opposite the forbidden color. This gives a balanced satisfying signing here too. Delete any dummy to finish.

**Why the proof stops at four.** With five relevant goods and u(A)=|A∩S|, receiving just one of the five can still fail EF2: four relevant goods are on the other side and two remain after deletion. Every candidate triple can be hit by such a failing bundle. This refutes the one-triple reduction, not the fairness guarantee for five supported goods. A ten-agent counterexample, if one exists, needs at least one agent whose valuation cannot be supported on four goods.
